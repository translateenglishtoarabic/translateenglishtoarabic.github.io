Getting Started

Arabic for Beginners: A Friendly Starting Guide

Arabic is the language of more than 400 million people across North Africa and the Middle East — and one of the six official languages of the United Nations. It can look intimidating at first, with its flowing right-to-left script and sounds that English does not have. But Arabic is also one of the most logical languages you can learn: its words are built from tidy root patterns, its spelling is consistent, and a small core vocabulary carries you surprisingly far.

One language, many voices

The first thing to understand is that "Arabic" comes in layers. Modern Standard Arabic (often shortened to MSA) is the shared written language of news broadcasts, books, official documents and this translator. Alongside it, every region speaks its own everyday dialect — Egyptian, Levantine, Gulf, Maghrebi and others. The smart strategy for a beginner is to start with MSA: everyone understands it, it unlocks reading, and you can pick up local dialect words later wherever you travel or settle.

How Arabic sounds

Arabic has a rich set of consonants, including deep "emphatic" sounds made at the back of the throat and the famous letter ع (ayn), which has no English equivalent. Do not let that worry you — Arabic speakers are famously patient with learners, and a cheerful مرحبا (marhaban — hello) is understood everywhere. Most everyday vocabulary uses sounds you already know.

The script is friendlier than it looks

Arabic has 28 letters, written from right to left, and most letters join to their neighbours like handwriting. That joining is the whole secret: each letter simply changes shape slightly depending on whether it stands at the start, middle or end of a word. Our alphabet guide walks through all 28 letters calmly, one group at a time.

The first words to learn

Start with survival words: مرحبا (marhaban — hello), شكرا (shukran — thank you), ماء (ma' — water), طعام (ta'am — food), كم الثمن؟ (kam ath-thaman? — how much is it?) and أين؟ (ayna? — where?). Add the numbers one to ten and the question words, and you can already handle taxis, cafés and market stalls. Our everyday vocabulary guide gives you sixty of the most useful words in one place.

A realistic study routine

Fifteen minutes a day beats a monthly marathon. Spend five minutes on the script, five on new words, and five speaking aloud — even to yourself. Use the translator on this site to check sentences about your own life: your family, your job, your town. Personal sentences stick in memory far better than textbook ones.

Above all, speak from the first week. A single السلام عليكم (as-salamu alaykum — peace be upon you) offered in a Cairo café or a Dubai shop will teach you more about Arab hospitality than a chapter of grammar. Ahlan wa sahlan — welcome to the language.

The Script

The Arabic Alphabet: 28 Letters and How They Join

Arabic writing flows from right to left in a connected, cursive style — and once you understand its one big idea, it stops looking mysterious. The idea is this: Arabic has only 28 letters, and almost every letter changes its shape depending on where it sits in a word. Learn the letters and their shapes, and you can sound out any word in the language, even one you have never seen before.

The 28 letters

Here are the letters in their traditional order, with the sound each one makes: ا a (alif), ب b, ت t, ث th (as in "think"), ج j, ح a deep h, خ kh (like clearing your throat), د d, ذ dh (as in "this"), ر r, ز z, س s, ش sh, ص a heavy s, ض a heavy d, ط a heavy t, ظ a heavy dh, ع ayn (a deep throat sound), غ gh, ف f, ق q (a deep k), ك k, ل l, م m, ن n, ه h and و w, ي y. Count them: 28.

Four shapes, one letter

Most letters take up to four forms: isolated (standing alone), initial (start of a word), medial (middle) and final (end). Take the letter b: alone it is ب, at the start بـ, in the middle ـبـ, and at the end ـب. The shapes look different, but the core of the letter — its dots and its basic stroke — stays recognisable. Dots matter enormously: ب (b, one dot below), ت (t, two dots above) and ث (th, three dots above) share one body with different dots.

The letters that will not join

Six letters — ا د ذ ر ز و — connect to the letter before them but refuse to connect forward to the next one. That is why some Arabic words appear to break into pieces: the word for book, كتاب (kitaab), shows a small gap after the alif. Expect these little breaks and reading becomes much easier.

Where are the vowels?

Long vowels are full letters: ا (aa), و (oo) and ي (ee). Short vowels are small marks written above or below letters — called harakat — and everyday Arabic (signs, newspapers, messages) usually leaves them out. Readers supply the vowels from context, the way English readers understand "rd ths sntnc". Children's books and the Quran print every mark, which makes them excellent practice material for beginners.

How to learn it

Learn the letters in small groups, writing each one in all its positions. Then read real things — shop signs, product labels, headlines — and check doubtful words in the translator. Within a few weeks, Arabic script stops being decoration and starts being information.

Better Results

How to Translate English to Arabic Accurately

Machine translation between English and Arabic has improved enormously, but the two languages are built very differently — one reads left to right, the other right to left; one marks gender everywhere, the other barely at all. Small habits on your side make a big difference to the result. These nine tips come from the errors we see most often.

1. Know which Arabic you are getting

This translator produces Modern Standard Arabic — the formal, shared Arabic of writing, news and business, understood from Morocco to Iraq. That is the right choice for messages, documents and signs. If you need casual spoken dialect for one specific country, expect locals to still understand MSA perfectly well; they will simply hear it as polite and formal.

2. Write complete sentences

Fragments confuse translators because Arabic verb forms carry information English leaves to context. "Going tomorrow" could be a question, a plan or a command. Write "I am going tomorrow" and the Arabic comes out clean: سأذهب غدا (sa-adhhab ghadan).

3. Keep sentences short

A forty-word English sentence with three clauses becomes tangled in any language pair. Split long sentences into two or three short ones before translating; the Arabic output will be dramatically more natural and much easier to check.

4. Use punctuation properly

Commas and full stops tell the translator where ideas end. Arabic uses the same full stop and a reversed comma (،) and question mark (؟), and the translator places them correctly when your English punctuation is clear. A missing full stop can glue two thoughts into one strange sentence.

5. Avoid slang and idioms

"It's raining cats and dogs" translated word by word produces very confused Arabic. Rewrite idioms plainly first — "it is raining heavily" — then translate. The meaning survives; the confusion does not.

6. Be careful with names and places

Translators sometimes translate names that happen to be ordinary words, or transliterate them inconsistently. If a person's or company's name must stay in English letters, translate the sentence in two parts and paste the name back in afterwards.

7. Watch the gender

Arabic marks gender in verbs, pronouns and adjectives — English does not. "You are welcome" said to a man and to a woman can come out differently, and "my friend" becomes صديقي (sadeeqi — male friend) or صديقتي (sadeeqati — female friend). When the result matters, add a hint in your English — "(to a woman)" in a draft note for yourself — and choose the form that fits the person you are addressing.

8. Do not worry about missing short vowels

The Arabic you receive will usually have no short-vowel marks, because that is how Arabic is normally written. Native readers expect this. If you are learning to read aloud, use the transliterations in our phrasebook to hear how the words sound.

9. Back-translate to check

Finally, paste the Arabic result back into the translator in the Arabic-to-English direction. If the English that returns matches what you meant, your translation is safe to send. Try it now on the translator — and keep our travel phrases handy if your translation is for a trip.

Travel

Essential Arabic Phrases for Travelling in the Arab World

One Arabic phrasebook covers an enormous map — from the pyramids of Egypt to the towers of Dubai, the holy cities of Saudi Arabia and the medinas of Morocco. English is widely spoken in hotels and cities, but a greeting in Arabic at a café, a taxi rank or a souk stall changes the whole conversation. Here are the phrases that matter most, grouped by the moments you will actually use them.

Greetings and courtesy

The greeting you will hear everywhere is السلام عليكم (as-salamu alaykum — peace be upon you); answer وعليكم السلام (wa alaykum as-salam — and upon you peace). A simple مرحبا (marhaban — hello) works anywhere too. Ask كيف حالك؟ (kayfa haluk? — how are you?) and answer أنا بخير (ana bikhayr — I am fine). Thank everyone with شكرا (shukran); it is short, sweet and always noticed.

Getting around

For taxis: كم الثمن؟ (kam ath-thaman? — how much is it?) settles the fare before you move in places where haggling is normal. توقف هنا من فضلك (tawaqqaf huna min fadlik — stop here, please), انعطف يسارا (in'atif yasaaran — turn left) and انعطف يمينا (in'atif yameenan — turn right) handle directions, and اذهب مباشرة (idhhab mubasharatan — go straight) keeps you on course.

Food and drink

Arab hospitality revolves around coffee and tea — you will be offered قهوة (qahwa — coffee) constantly, and accepting is polite. Ask for ماء (ma' — water) freely in the heat, declare أنا نباتي (ana nabaati — I am vegetarian) if needed, and praise the food with إنه لذيذ (innahu latheeth — it is delicious). Your host will beam.

Shopping and bargaining

كم الثمن؟ (kam ath-thaman? — how much is this?) opens every negotiation in the souk. If the price feels high, smile and try again later — bargaining is a friendly ritual, not a fight. Finish with سآخذ هذا (sa-aakhudh haadha — I will take this) when you are happy. Our shopping guide has the full script.

Emergencies and help

Memorise three lines just in case: النجدة! (an-najda! — help!), اتصل بطبيب (ittasel bi-tabeeb — call a doctor) and أين المستشفى؟ (ayna al-mustashfa? — where is the hospital?). People across the Arab world are quick to help a traveller in trouble — often before you finish the sentence.

Save this page, copy the phrases you need from our phrasebook, and use the translator for anything else that comes up on the road. رحلة سعيدة! (rihla saeeda! — have a happy journey!)

Numbers & Counting

Arabic Numbers 1 to 100: Counting Made Simple

Numbers are among the first things you need in any language. In the Arab world you will use them for taxi fares, souk prices, hotel rooms, phone numbers and train times — often several times a day. Arabic counting has a few quirks, but once you know the first ten, the rest follows a learnable pattern.

The numbers 1 to 10

Everything starts here: واحد (wahed — one), اثنان (ithnan — two), ثلاثة (thalatha — three), أربعة (arba'a — four), خمسة (khamsa — five), ستة (sitta — six), سبعة (sab'a — seven), ثمانية (thamaniya — eight), تسعة (tis'a — nine) and عشرة (ashara — ten). Say them aloud a few times; vendors will recognise your effort instantly.

The tens: 20 to 100

The tens are: عشرون (ishroon — twenty), ثلاثون (thalathoon — thirty), أربعون (arba'oon — forty), خمسون (khamsoon — fifty), ستون (sittoon — sixty), سبعون (sab'oon — seventy), ثمانون (thamanoon — eighty), تسعون (tis'oon — ninety) and مئة (mi'a — one hundred). Numbers in between put the unit first, then "and", then the ten: twenty-five is خمسة وعشرون (khamsa wa ishroon — literally "five and twenty").

Hundreds and thousands

For bigger numbers: مئة (mi'a — one hundred), مئتان (mi'atan — two hundred) and ألف (alf — one thousand). You may know "alf" already from "Alf Layla wa Layla" — the Thousand and One Nights. Zero is صفر (sifr) — the word that travelled into English as "cipher" and "zero" itself comes from the same root.

Two sets of digits

Here is the surprise: the digits 0 1 2 3 that the world calls "Arabic numerals" are indeed the ones used in Morocco, and across much of the region for prices and phones. But Egypt, Saudi Arabia and the Gulf often write numbers with the older Arabic-Indic digits: ٠١٢٣٤٥٦٧٨٩ (0–9). So ٢٥ is twenty-five in Cairo. Both systems are easy to learn, and recognising them will save you real confusion at market stalls and on licence plates.

Numbers in daily life

A few ready-made lines cover most situations. Asking a price: كم الثمن؟ (kam ath-thaman? — how much is it?). For quantities, put the number after the thing counted in careful Arabic, though in shops a number plus the item will always be understood: شايان (shaayan — two teas) uses the special "dual" form Arabic has for exactly two of something.

Practise by counting real things this week — steps, stops, cups of coffee — and test any number sentence in the translator before you use it. Numbers plus the phrases in our shopping guide make market day much easier.

How to use this list

Do not try to memorise all sixty in one sitting. Stick the words on sticky notes around your home — ma' on the tap, baab on the door, kitaab on a book — and your kitchen becomes a classroom. When a word will not stick, look at its root: shajara (tree), qamar (moon) and their cousins reappear inside dozens of other words, so every word you learn makes the next one easier.

Keep this list beside our phrasebook, and drop any word into the translator to see it inside a full sentence. Sixty words will not make you fluent — but they will make the Arab world feel a lot less foreign.

Time & Dates

Days, Months and Telling the Time in Arabic

"When?" is one of the most practical questions in any language. Whether you are catching a train, fixing a meeting or planning around Ramadan, you need days, dates and clock times. Arabic keeps this manageable: a small set of words covers almost every plan you will make.

The seven days

The week in most Arab countries runs: الاثنين (al-ithnayn — Monday), الثلاثاء (ath-thulatha — Tuesday), الأربعاء (al-arbi'a — Wednesday), الخميس (al-khamees — Thursday), الجمعة (al-jum'a — Friday), السبت (as-sabt — Saturday) and الأحد (al-ahad — Sunday). Several names are built from numbers — Wednesday is literally "the fourth" — and Friday, al-jum'a, is the weekly day of prayer and rest across the region.

Today, tomorrow and yesterday

Three words handle most planning: اليوم (al-yawm — today), غدا (ghadan — tomorrow) and أمس (ams — yesterday). "See you tomorrow" is أراك غدا (araka ghadan) — a phrase worth using daily.

Parts of the day

The day divides into صباح (sabah — morning), ظهر (dhuhr — midday), مساء (masa — evening) and ليل (layl — night). Greetings follow the same pattern — صباح الخير (sabah al-khayr) for good morning, answered charmingly with صباح النور (sabah an-noor — "morning of light").

Months of the year — two calendars

For everyday dates, the Arab world uses the familiar Gregorian months, often with Levantine names like كانون الثاني (kanoon ath-thani — January) and آب (aab — August) in the east, or European-derived names in the west. Alongside it runs the Islamic Hijri calendar, whose months govern religious life: محرم (Muharram) opens the year, رمضان (Ramadan) is the fasting month, شوال (Shawwal) brings Eid al-Fitr, and ذو الحجة (Dhu al-Hijja) is the month of Hajj and Eid al-Adha. Because the Hijri year is about eleven days shorter, Ramadan slowly travels through all the seasons.

Asking and telling the time

Ask كم الساعة؟ (kam as-saa'a? — what time is it?). Answers use الساعة (as-saa'a — "the hour is"): الساعة العاشرة (as-saa'a al-aashira — ten o'clock). An hour is ساعة (saa'a) and a minute is دقيقة (daqeeqa). "Half past" is والنصف (wan-nisf).

Seasons and useful time phrases

The four seasons are الربيع (ar-rabee' — spring), الصيف (as-sayf — summer), الخريف (al-khareef — autumn) and الشتاء (ash-shitaa' — winter). A handful of phrases rounds out your planning vocabulary: الأسبوع القادم (al-usboo' al-qaadim — next week), الشهر الماضي (ash-shahr al-maadi — last month), في الصباح (fis-sabah — in the morning), بعد الظهر (ba'd adh-dhuhr — in the afternoon) and قريبا (qareeban — soon). With these and the days above, you can fix appointments, read event posters and follow the small print of any itinerary in the region.

Combine these with the numbers guide and you can read any timetable in the region. For longer sentences, draft them in the translator and check the word order before you travel.

Family

Family Words in Arabic: Relatives and Relationships

Family sits at the centre of life across the Arab world, and Arabic has a precise word for almost every relationship — including a distinction English never makes between your father's brother and your mother's brother. Learning these words does more than build vocabulary: it explains the warmth of Arab introductions, where asking after someone's family is not small talk but genuine courtesy.

Parents and grandparents

Father is أب (ab) — in everyday speech often بابا (baba) — and mother is أم (umm), affectionately ماما (mama). Grandfather is جد (jadd) and grandmother is جدة (jadda). The general word for family itself is عائلة (a'ila), and the wider clan or extended family is أهل (ahl) — as in "Ahlan wa sahlan", where ahlan literally welcomes you "to family".

Brothers and sisters

A brother is أخ (akh) and a sister is أخت (ukht). These words stretch far beyond the household: men address friends and even strangers as "akhi" (my brother) and women as "ukhti" (my sister), which is why you will hear them constantly in shops and streets. Elder and younger are added when needed — الأخ الأكبر (al-akh al-akbar — the elder brother).

Uncles and aunts

Here Arabic is more detailed than English. Your father's brother is عم (amm) and his wife or your father's sister is عمة (amma). Your mother's brother is خال (khaal) and your mother's sister is خالة (khaala). Children use these titles for respected older neighbours too — a kind "amm" to an older man is never wrong.

Children and the next generation

A son is ابن (ibn) and a daughter is ابنة (ibna); a child is طفل (tifl) and children are أطفال (atfaal). A husband is زوج (zawj) and a wife is زوجة (zawja). Grandchildren are أحفاد (ahfaad).

Cousins, in-laws and family occasions

Arabic names cousins by their exact branch: your paternal uncle's son is ابن العم (ibn al-amm) and your maternal aunt's daughter is بنت الخالة (bint al-khaala) — a precision that reflects how close these relationships are in daily life. In-laws have their own words too: a father-in-law is حمو (hamu) and a mother-in-law is حماة (hamaa). You will hear family vocabulary at full volume during رمضان (Ramadan) iftars and Eid visits, when three or four generations gather around one table — our festivals guide explains those occasions and the greetings that go with them.

Talking about your family

Two sentence frames cover most introductions. "This is my mother" is هذه أمي (haadhihi ummi). "I have two brothers" is لدي أخوان (ladayya akhawaan). Notice how "my" is a small ending stuck onto the word — umm becomes ummi, akh becomes akhi; both return in our self-introduction guide.

Family questions are friendly conversation in the Arab world, not prying — asking about someone's عائلة shows you care. Practise your answers in the translator, and you will be ready for the warmest small talk in the region.

Speaking

Arabic Greetings and Goodbyes for Every Occasion

Every conversation in the Arab world begins and ends with warmth, and the words are easy to learn. Master this small set of greetings and goodbyes and you will never be stuck at the start or the end of a chat again — in a home, a shop, an office or on the phone.

The greeting of peace

The most important greeting in Arabic is السلام عليكم (as-salamu alaykum — peace be upon you). It is used at any hour, in any country, in formal and friendly settings alike. The reply is just as fixed: وعليكم السلام (wa alaykum as-salam — and upon you be peace), sometimes extended with ورحمة الله وبركاته (wa rahmatullah wa barakatuh — and God's mercy and blessings). Offer the greeting first when you enter a room or a shop; it is the done thing, and it is always appreciated.

Everyday hellos

Alongside it, مرحبا (marhaban — hello) is the neutral, all-purpose greeting, and أهلا وسهلا (ahlan wa sahlan) is the warm welcome — literally "you have come to family and to ease". You will hear ahlan on its own constantly, as hello, welcome and "make yourself at home" in one word.

Greetings by time of day

Arabic has beautiful time-specific greetings that come in call-and-response pairs: صباح الخير (sabah al-khayr — good morning, "morning of goodness") is answered صباح النور (sabah an-noor — "morning of light"), and مساء الخير (masa al-khayr — good evening) is answered مساء النور (masa an-noor). Good night is تصبح على خير (tusbih ala khayr — "may you wake to goodness").

Asking how someone is

The standard question is كيف حالك؟ (kayfa haluk? — how are you?). The usual answer is أنا بخير، الحمد لله (ana bikhayr, alhamdulillah — I am fine, praise be to God) — the alhamdulillah appears in replies everywhere, in good news and bad. Among friends you will also hear ما أخبارك؟ (ma akhbaruk? — what's your news?).

Welcome and congratulations

Congratulate with مبروك (mabrook — congratulations, blessed), wish a birthday with عيد ميلاد سعيد (eid milad saeed), and thank someone warmly with شكرا جزيلا (shukran jazeelan — thank you very much). The reply to shukran is عفوا (afwan — you're welcome). Festival wishes get their own guide — see our festivals article.

Saying goodbye — Arab style

The classic parting is مع السلامة (ma'a as-salama — "go with safety"), said by the person staying; the person leaving often says في أمان الله (fi aman illah — "in God's protection") or simply إلى اللقاء (ila al-liqa — until we meet). Goodbyes in Arab homes can take a while — at the door, at the gate, at the car — and that is exactly as it should be.

Try one greeting today — at a shop, with a neighbour, anywhere — and build the habit with the phrasebook. For meeting someone properly for the first time, continue to our self-introduction guide.

Shopping

Shopping and Bargaining in Arabic: Souk Phrases That Work

The old markets of the Arab world — the souks of Cairo, Damascus, Marrakech and Dubai's Deira — run on friendly talk. Prices in small shops and stalls are often a conversation, not a label, and bargaining done with humour is a social ritual both sides enjoy. These phrases will help you shop with confidence, from the first ahlan to the final shukran.

Entering and looking around

Start with السلام عليكم (as-salamu alaykum) — in a souk, the greeting comes before business. If you are just browsing, say أنا أتفرج فقط (ana atafarraj faqat — I am just looking). To ask for something specific: أرني هذا من فضلك (arini haadha min fadlik — please show me this), or هل لديك لون آخر؟ (hal ladayka lawn akhar? — do you have another colour?). The word for shop is متجر (matjar) and for market, سوق (sooq).

Asking the price

The one question you will use everywhere: كم الثمن؟ (kam ath-thaman? — how much is this?). For things sold by weight, ask بكم الكيلو؟ (bikam al-keelu? — how much per kilo?). Answers come fast, so learn the numbers first. If you did not catch the reply, أعد من فضلك (a'id min fadlik — please repeat) buys you a slower second hearing.

Sizes, colours and trying things

For clothes and footwear: هل لديك مقاس أكبر؟ (hal ladayka maqaas akbar? — do you have a bigger size?) and مقاس أصغر (maqaas asghar — a smaller size). Common colours: أحمر (ahmar — red), أزرق (azraq — blue), أخضر (akhdar — green), أسود (aswad — black), أبيض (abyad — white) and أصفر (asfar — yellow).

Bargaining, politely

In souks (less so in malls and fixed-price shops), gentle bargaining is expected. Open with هذا غال جدا (haadha ghaalin jiddan — that is very expensive), then offer: هل يمكنك تخفيض الثمن؟ (hal yumkinuk takhfeed ath-thaman? — can you lower the price?). Tea may appear; accept it — the negotiation is going well. Accept a deal with اتفقنا (ittafaqna — agreed!), or decline kindly with لا، شكرا (la, shukran — no, thank you) and a smile.

Paying and finishing

Ask for the bill with الفاتورة من فضلك (al-fatura min fadlik). Cash is نقدا (naqdan); most city shops take cards, while small souk stalls often prefer cash. When you buy something, the seller may say مبروك (mabrook — congratulations on your purchase) — the correct, happy reply is الله يبارك فيك (allah yubaarik feek — may God bless you).

Keep this guide open on market day alongside the phrasebook, and run any tricky sentence through the translator before you set out. Bargaining in Arabic, even imperfect Arabic, changes the whole shopping experience.

Emergency

Emergency and Health Phrases in Arabic

Nobody plans an emergency, but a few memorised lines can make a frightening moment manageable. The Arab world has good hospitals in every major city and helpful people everywhere; these phrases make sure you can ask for the right help quickly, for yourself or for someone else. Save this page offline before you travel.

Calling for help

The word that cuts through noise is النجدة! (an-najda! — help!). Add خطر! (khatar! — danger!) if there has been a crash or a fall. To stop someone urgently: توقف! (tawaqqaf! — stop!). Emergency numbers vary by country — 112 works on mobile phones in many of them, Egypt uses 122 for police and 123 for ambulance, the UAE uses 999, and Saudi Arabia uses 911 in major regions — so save your destination's numbers in your phone alongside these words.

Getting medical help

The essentials: أين المستشفى؟ (ayna al-mustashfa? — where is the hospital?), اتصل بطبيب (ittasel bi-tabeeb — call a doctor), and اتصل بالإسعاف (ittasel bil-is'aaf — call an ambulance). The word for a doctor is طبيب (tabeeb) and for a hospital, مستشفى (mustashfa).

Describing what is wrong

Start with أنا لست بخير (ana lastu bikhayr — I am not well). The word ألم (alam — pain) combines with body parts: ألم في الرأس (alam fir-ra's — headache), ألم في البطن (alam fil-batn — stomach ache), ألم في الأسنان (alam fil-asnaan — toothache). Other common words: حمى (humma — fever), سعال (su'aal — cough), دم (dam — blood) and حساسية (hasaasiyya — allergy). "It hurts here" is يؤلمني هنا (yu'limuni huna) — point as you say it.

At the pharmacy

Medicine is دواء (dawaa), and a pharmacy is صيدلية (saydaliyya) — often marked with a green cross and open late. Useful lines: هل لديك هذا الدواء؟ (hal ladayka haadha ad-dawaa? — do you have this medicine?) and كم مرة في اليوم يجب أن آخذه؟ (kam marra fil-yawm yajib an aakhudhahu? — how many times a day should I take it?). Pharmacists across the region are highly trained and often speak English.

Police and personal safety

For theft or trouble: اتصل بالشرطة (ittasel bish-shurta — call the police). "I have lost my bag" is لقد فقدت حقيبتي (laqad faqadtu haqeebati), and "I don't understand" — أنا لا أفهم (ana la afham) — is the honest phrase that slows any difficult conversation down to a manageable speed.

Grammar Basics

Arabic Question Words and Pronouns: Ask Anything

Questions are the engine of conversation. Once you can ask what, where, when and how much in Arabic, every street, shop and café becomes a classroom. This guide collects the complete question toolkit — the question words, the yes-or-no question trick, and the personal pronouns you need to aim your questions at the right person.

The question words

  • ما؟ / ماذا؟ (ma? / maadha?) — what?
  • من؟ (man?) — who?
  • أين؟ (ayna?) — where?
  • متى؟ (mata?) — when?
  • لماذا؟ (limadha?) — why?
  • كيف؟ (kayfa?) — how?
  • كم؟ (kam?) — how much? how many?
  • أي؟ (ayy?) — which?
  • هل؟ (hal?) — the yes-or-no question word

The magic word: hal

Arabic has a gift for learners: put هل (hal) at the start of any statement and it becomes a yes-or-no question. هذا قريب (haadha qareeb — this is near) becomes هل هذا قريب؟ (hal haadha qareeb? — is this near?). One small word, endless questions. In everyday speech, rising intonation alone often does the same job.

The personal pronouns

  • أنا (ana) — I
  • أنت (anta) — you (to a man)
  • أنتِ (anti) — you (to a woman)
  • هو (huwa) — he
  • هي (hiya) — she
  • نحن (nahn) — we
  • أنتم (antum) — you (plural)
  • هم (hum) — they

Notice that Arabic distinguishes "you" by gender — anta to a man, anti to a woman — and often skips the pronoun entirely, because the verb ending already says who is acting. You will also meet these pronouns as endings glued to words: kitab (book) becomes كتابي (kitaabi — my book) and كتابك (kitaabuk — your book).

Building your first real questions

Combine and go: ما هذا؟ (ma haadha? — what is this?), أين السوق؟ (ayna as-sooq? — where is the market?), متى يفتح المتجر؟ (mata yaftah al-matjar? — when does the shop open?), كيف أذهب إلى المطار؟ (kayfa adhhab ila al-mataar? — how do I get to the airport?) and كم الساعة؟ (kam as-saa'a? — what time is it?). Each follows the same pattern: question word first, then the rest of the sentence.

Polite ways to open a question

A question lands better with a softener in front of it. Begin with من فضلك (min fadlik — please) or the gentle attention-getter عفوا (afwan — excuse me) before you ask a stranger anything: عفوا، أين المحطة؟ (afwan, ayna al-mahatta? — excuse me, where is the station?). To check whether someone speaks English, ask هل تتكلم الإنجليزية؟ (hal tatakallam al-ingleeziyya? — do you speak English?) — a hal question, and one that will rescue you regularly while your Arabic grows. And when the answer comes too fast, the honest rescue line is تكلم ببطء من فضلك (takallam bi-but' min fadlik — please speak slowly).

Practise by turning statements from the phrasebook into questions with hal, and test longer questions in the translator. Questions plus the greetings are the two halves of every friendly conversation in Arabic.

Culture

Ramadan, Eid and Arabic Festivals: Words and Wishes

Festivals shape the year across the Arab world, and each one comes with its own vocabulary and its own greetings. Even if you share none of the faith behind them, knowing the words — and sending the right wish at the right time — is one of the warmest ways to connect with Arab friends, colleagues and hosts.

Ramadan, the holy month

رمضان (Ramadan) is the ninth month of the Islamic calendar, when Muslims fast from dawn to sunset. Days grow quiet and reflective; nights come alive. The fast is broken at sunset with إفطار (iftaar) — often starting with dates and water, then a feast with family — and a pre-dawn meal called سحور (suhoor) starts the next fast. The greeting of the month is رمضان كريم (ramadan kareem — "generous Ramadan"), answered with الله أكرم (allahu akram — "God is more generous"). Visiting during Ramadan? Avoid eating in front of fasting people in public, accept iftar invitations with delight, and expect the best food of the year.

Eid al-Fitr, the festival of breaking the fast

When Ramadan ends, عيد الفطر (Eid al-Fitr) begins: three days of new clothes, family visits, sweets like kahk and ma'amoul, and gifts of money (eidiyya) for children. The universal greeting is عيد مبارك (eid mubarak — "blessed Eid"), and you will hear it a hundred times a day. Another classic wish is كل عام وأنتم بخير (kull aam wa antum bikhayr — "may you be well every year"), which works for both Eids and the New Year too.

Eid al-Adha, the festival of sacrifice

About two months later comes عيد الأضحى (Eid al-Adha), the greater Eid, coinciding with the Hajj pilgrimage to Makkah. Families share meat with neighbours and the poor, and the same greetings — eid mubarak above all — fill the streets. If a friend is performing Hajj, wish them حج مبرور (hajj mabroor — an accepted pilgrimage) when they return.

Other dates in the year

المولد النبوي (al-Mawlid an-Nabawi) marks the Prophet Muhammad's birthday, celebrated with processions and sweets in many countries. The Islamic New Year begins with محرم (Muharram). In Egypt, شم النسيم (Sham an-Naseem) — a spring festival older than any current religion — sends everyone to parks with coloured eggs and salted fish the day after Coptic Easter. National days, from Saudi National Day on 23 September to UAE National Day on 2 December, add fireworks and flags to the calendar.

Sending the wishes

Keep it simple and sincere. For Ramadan: رمضان كريم (ramadan kareem). For either Eid: عيد مبارك (eid mubarak) or عيد سعيد (eid saeed — happy Eid). For almost any celebration: كل عام وأنتم بخير (kull aam wa antum bikhayr). Type your own message into the translator to build a longer, personal greeting around these anchors.

Festivals are when the Arab world shows its most generous face. Learn the wishes, accept the sweets, and read our greetings guide for the everyday courtesies that carry you the rest of the year.

Speaking

How to Introduce Yourself in Arabic

Introductions in the Arab world are unhurried and warm: a greeting, a name, a handshake or a hand on the heart, and a few questions that show genuine interest. With the frames in this guide you can present yourself properly in Arabic in under a minute — and understand the questions that come back at you.

Step 1 — Greet first, always

Never open with your name. Open with السلام عليكم (as-salamu alaykum — peace be upon you) and answer any greeting you receive with وعليكم السلام (wa alaykum as-salam). A smile and, among friends of the same gender, a handshake complete the opening. Our greetings guide covers the full etiquette.

Step 2 — Say your name

The frame is اسمي (ismi — my name is) plus your name: اسمي سارة (ismi Sara — my name is Sara). To ask theirs: ما اسمك؟ (ma ismuk? — what is your name?). Follow with تشرفنا (tasharrafna — honoured to meet you) — the classic, gracious reply that Arabs use exactly where English says "pleased to meet you".

Step 3 — Say where you are from

Use أنا من (ana min — I am from): أنا من باكستان (ana min Pakistan — I am from Pakistan), أنا من لندن (ana min London — I am from London). For the city where you live now, add أعيش في (a'eesh fi — I live in): أعيش في دبي (a'eesh fi Dubai — I live in Dubai).

Step 4 — Age, family and work

Age uses the word for years: عمري ثلاثون سنة (umri thalathoon sana — I am thirty years old, literally "my age is thirty years"). Work is أعمل (a'mal — I work as): أعمل مهندسا (a'mal muhandisan — I work as an engineer), أعمل معلما (a'mal mu'alliman — I work as a teacher). Students say أنا طالب (ana taalib — I am a student) or أنا طالبة (ana taaliba) if female. Family words — if you want them — are all in our family guide.

A full example

Put it together: السلام عليكم. اسمي أحمد، وأنا من مصر. أعيش في القاهرة، وأعمل مهندسا. عمري ثلاثون سنة. تشرفنا! (as-salamu alaykum. ismi Ahmed, wa ana min Misr. a'eesh fil-Qahira, wa a'mal muhandisan. umri thalathoon sana. tasharrafna!) — "Peace be upon you. My name is Ahmed, and I am from Egypt. I live in Cairo, and I work as an engineer. I am thirty years old. Honoured to meet you!"

Now build your own version with the translator: write your introduction in English, translate it, and practise it aloud until it feels like yours. Then all that remains is to use it — and Arab hospitality will do the rest.

Food & Drink

Arabic for Food Lovers: Eating Your Way Across the Arab World

Food is the Arab world's love language. Meals are generous, shared and unhurried, and knowing the names of the great dishes — plus a few phrases for ordering and praising — turns eating from a necessity into the best part of your trip. This guide is a tour of the table, region by region.

The dishes everyone should know

Start with the classics that appear from Amman to Riyadh. حمص (hummus) — chickpea dip with tahini — and its smoky cousin made from aubergine, متبل (mutabbal), open most Levantine meals alongside warm خبز (khubz — bread). فلافل (falafel) — crisp fried balls of chickpea or fava bean — is the region's favourite street food. شاورما (shawarma) needs no introduction: marinated meat shaved from a turning spit into bread with pickles and garlic sauce. In the Gulf, the celebration dish is كبسة (kabsa) — fragrant spiced rice with chicken or lamb, served on a platter for sharing — and Jordan answers with منسف (mansaf), lamb in dried-yoghurt sauce over rice. Save room for كنافة (kunafa), the molten-cheese dessert under shredded pastry and syrup that has started arguments between cities claiming to have invented it.

Ordering like a local

A restaurant is a مطعم (mat'am) and the menu is القائمة (al-qa'ima). Order with أريد (ureed — I would like): أريد كبسة من فضلك (ureed kabsa min fadlik — I would like a kabsa, please). Ask ما هو الطبق الخاص اليوم؟ (ma huwa at-tabaq al-khaas al-yawm? — what is today's special?) for the dish the kitchen is proudest of. Drinks: شاي (shay — tea, often with mint or sage) and قهوة (qahwa — coffee, traditionally unsweetened and cardamom-scented in the Gulf) will arrive whether you order them or not.

Needs and preferences

Say أنا نباتي (ana nabaati — I am vegetarian) early; kitchens will happily build a meal around the region's superb vegetable and lentil dishes. For allergies, use our emergency guide's word حساسية (hasaasiyya). "Without sugar" is بدون سكر (bidoon sukkar) — essential for the famously sweet tea — and "a little spicy" is حار قليلا (haar qaleelan).

Praising the food (and the cook)

Compliments matter. إنه لذيذ (innahu latheeth — it is delicious) is the basic line; level up with أشهى طعام ذقته (ashha ta'am dhuqtuh — the tastiest food I have had). In a home, thank the cook with تسلم يداك (taslam yadaak — "may your hands be safe"), the traditional blessing for the person who cooked. When you are full — truly full, because more will be offered — الحمد لله، شبعت (alhamdulillah, shabi't — praise God, I am full) is the graceful way to stop.

Ask for the bill with الفاتورة من فضلك (al-fatura min fadlik — the bill, please), and keep the phrasebook open beside your plate. Hungry for more words? Our vocabulary guide has a full food section to revise between meals.

How Arabic Sentences Are Built: Word Order, Gender and Plurals

Once you can spot how Arabic sentences are assembled, the whole language becomes less mysterious. Arabic grammar has a reputation for complexity, but its foundations rest on a few clear ideas — and two of them will feel easier than English, not harder. This guide lays out the architecture; keep it beside the translator and test every pattern with your own sentences.

Pattern one: the verb comes first

A classic Arabic sentence opens with the verb: كتب الطالب الدرس (kataba at-taalib ad-dars — "wrote the student the lesson", i.e. the student wrote the lesson). This verb–subject–object order is the signature of formal Arabic. Modern writing also uses the English-like subject-first order freely, which is why the translator's output may start with the subject — both are correct, and Arabs move between them without thinking.

Pattern two: the sentence with no verb at all

Arabic has no present-tense word for "is" or "are". Instead it simply places subject and description side by side: البيت كبير (al-bayt kabeer — "the house big", meaning the house is big). These are called nominal sentences, and they are everywhere. The word "was" does exist — كان (kaana) — so the past tense gets a verb while the present does without one.

Every noun is masculine or feminine

Unlike English, Arabic gives every noun a gender. Masculine is the default; feminine nouns usually end in the letter ta marbuta (ة), pronounced "a": سيارة (sayyaara — car, feminine) versus بيت (bayt — house, masculine). Adjectives follow their noun and copy its gender: سيارة جديدة (sayyaara jadeeda — a new car), بيت جديد (bayt jadeed — a new house). This is also why "you" and "thank you" forms shift when spoken to a woman — the pattern runs through the whole language, as our accuracy guide explains.

Plurals: tidy ones and broken ones

Some plurals are regular — add an ending and you are done, as in معلمون (mu'allimoon — teachers). But Arabic is famous for "broken plurals", where the inside of the word reshapes itself: كتاب (kitaab — book) becomes كتب (kutub — books), and رجل (rajul — man) becomes رجال (rijaal — men). Do not memorise them one by one; you will absorb the common ones by reading, and the translator always chooses the right one for you.

The root system: Arabic's master key

Underneath everything sits the most elegant idea in the language: most words grow from a root of three consonants. The root ك ت ب (k-t-b) carries the idea of writing, and from it Arabic builds كتب (kataba — he wrote), كتاب (kitaab — book), كاتب (kaatib — writer), مكتبة (maktaba — library) and مكتب (maktab — office). Learn a root and you have learned a family. It is the single best reason to keep going after the beginner stage — vocabulary starts to organise itself.

Revisit our beginners guide with these patterns in mind and you will see them everywhere. That is when Arabic stops being a code to crack and becomes a system to enjoy.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is this English to Arabic translator free?

Yes. Translating, the phrasebook and all sixteen guides are completely free, with no sign-up and no account. The tool runs in your browser and gives you unlimited everyday translations of up to 2,000 characters at a time.

Which kind of Arabic does it produce?

Modern Standard Arabic (MSA) — the shared written form used in news, books, official documents and business across all Arab countries. It is understood everywhere from Morocco to Iraq. Spoken dialects differ by country, and our travel guide explains how MSA and dialect fit together.

Why does the Arabic text run right-to-left?

Arabic is written from right to left — the opposite direction to English. The translation box on this site sets the direction automatically, and when you copy Arabic into WhatsApp, email or a document, the text keeps its correct right-to-left layout because it is standard Unicode.

Can I translate Arabic back into English?

Yes. Click the round swap button between the language names on the home page. The input box switches to right-to-left for comfortable Arabic typing, and your Arabic text is translated into English on the other side.

How accurate is the translation?

For everyday sentences, messages and travel language it is very good, especially when you write complete, clearly punctuated sentences. For legal contracts, medical documents or anything where a single word carries serious consequences, have the result checked by a professional human translator.

Why are there no little vowel marks in the Arabic output?

Those marks (called harakat) show short vowels, but everyday Arabic — newspapers, signs, messages, books for adults — is normally written without them, and native readers supply the vowels from context. Children's books and the Quran print them in full. Our phrasebook gives you a pronunciation line in English letters for every phrase instead.

What is the character limit?

You can translate up to 2,000 characters at once — roughly a long paragraph or a full page of a letter. For longer documents, translate one paragraph at a time; you will usually get cleaner Arabic that way too.

Do you store the text I translate?

No. Your text is sent only to the translation service needed to translate it, and we keep no copy on any server. The Recent translations list is stored only in your own browser, on your own device, and you can clear it any time with the Clear button.

Can I use the translations in my own messages and posts?

Of course. Copy the Arabic into WhatsApp, Facebook, email or documents freely — that is exactly what the tool is for. The translations are standard Unicode Arabic and display correctly on phones and computers.

Where should a complete beginner start?

Start with the Arabic for Beginners guide, then the Arabic Alphabet guide to understand the script, and learn ten phrases a day from the phrasebook. Within a few weeks you will be reading signs and holding simple conversations.
